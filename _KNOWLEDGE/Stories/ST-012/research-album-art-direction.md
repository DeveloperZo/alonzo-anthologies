# Research: album-sleeve art direction for the career profile

Story: ST-012 (Career Profile). Format: one page, 1600x1000. Left: square cover (about 1000x1000 with margin). Right: back of the sleeve (tracklist and liner notes).

Constraints carried through every direction:

- Blue-led, calm, quirky. Never the dark page with an orange accent, never gradient washes, never stat tiles.
- No "lofi girl at a rainy window". The current rainy-window sketch should be retired.
- Pure HTML, CSS and inline SVG. Google Fonts only. No external images or scripts.

## Part 1: references and what makes them work

### Blue Note and Reid Miles (1956 to 1967)

- Miles joined Blue Note in the mid 1950s and designed several hundred covers. Francis Wolff supplied the session photographs ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Album_covers_of_Blue_Note_Records), [Typeroom](https://www.typeroom.eu/article/reid-miles)).
- What works:
  - Type is the image. Miles set titles huge, stretched, stacked or cropped off the edge, often with exclamation marks.
  - Photographs were tinted a single colour and cropped hard.
  - Many covers used only one or two inks on cream or black, which forces the composition to do the work.
  - Many later covers had no photograph at all. That is the license for a typography-led cover here.
- Lesson for Zo: one ink colour plus black, and a title that fills a third of the square.

### Madvillainy (Madvillain, Stones Throw, 2004)

- Jeff Jank designed it from an Eric Coleman photo taken in the house where the record was made ([Red Bull interview](https://redbull.com/gb-en/under-the-covers-stones-throw-jeff-jank-interview), [Daily Maverick](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2021-01-19-madvillainy-the-2004-underground-hip-hop-album-by-us-duo-madvillain-that-still-resonates.md)).
- What works:
  - A tight square crop of one face, in black and white.
  - A single small orange square stands in for the whole title. Jank cited Madonna's debut as a nod, and said the design needed one distinctive thing.
- Lesson: one small flat geometric mark in a single colour, placed off-centre, carries more identity than a headline. Here it should be blue, not orange.

### J Dilla, Donuts (Stones Throw, 2006)

- Jank designed both covers: a candid smiling portrait, and an LP alternate drawn as a donut shop ([Stones Throw](https://www.stonesthrow.com/news/behind-the-smile-j-dilla-donuts/)).
- What works: it is warm and unposed, and the alternate cover is a plain drawing of an everyday place, with no "music" imagery at all.
- Lesson: the cover can show an ordinary object or place that means something to the person. It does not need instruments, vinyl or headphones.

### Nujabes and Hydeout Productions

- The People's Graphic Design Archive credits the Metaphorical Music design to SYU (reported as Shuichi Nagai), who designed other Nujabes sleeves ([PGDA](https://peoplesgdarchive.org/item/16981/metaphorical-music)).
- What works: a light illustration floating on white space, with small, quiet type.
- Lesson: these are the real roots of "lofi jazz hop". They are airy and white, not dark and rainy. Quoting the restraint, rather than the meme, is the honest homage.

### Japanese city pop and jazz sleeves

- Hiroshi Nagai's covers for Eiichi Ohtaki's A Long Vacation (1981) and others defined city pop ([Japan Foundation Sydney](https://sydney.jpf.go.jp/events/hiroshi-nagai-paintings-for-music), [The Vinyl Factory](https://www.thevinylfactory.com/features/japanese-illustrator-hiroshi-nagai-cover-art)).
  - He lays down blue, then airbrushes white up from the horizon, so the sky is the subject ([Uniqlo UT](https://www.uniqlo.com/jp/en/contents/feature/ut-magazine/amp/s137/)).
- Three Blind Mice (founded 1970) is a Japanese jazz label known for audiophile pressings. Its reissues come with obi strips ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Blind_Mice_(record_label))).
- What works:
  - Flat, airless blue fields.
  - The obi: a vertical paper band wrapped round the sleeve edge, carrying the catalogue number and a short line of copy.
- Lesson: the obi strip is a ready-made, culturally specific quirk. It also does a real job, as a place for a one-line pitch.

### ECM Records (Barbara Wojirsch, then Dieter Rehm)

- Wojirsch shaped ECM's look from 1970 and designed more than 200 covers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_Wojirsch), [Eye](https://eyemagazine.com/feature/article/one-man-brand), [Substack essay](https://theartofcoverart.substack.com/p/the-designer-behind-the-visual-identity)).
  - She stripped each cover down to "a thought or a gesture".
  - Typography is slender and gives only the barest information.
  - Covers use muted colour and textural surfaces such as weather, water, fog and stone.
- What works: silence. Small type, a large empty field and one texture.
- Lesson: calm comes from empty space, not from a dim palette. ECM shows how to keep a weather image from turning sentimental.

### Unknown Pleasures and the CP 1919 pulsar plot

- Harold Craft's Cornell dissertation stacked 80 consecutive pulses from pulsar CP 1919, recorded at Arecibo. Peter Saville reversed the plot to white on black in 1979, with no band name and no title ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-pulsar-chart-that-became-a-pop-icon-turns-50-joy-division-rsquo-s-unknown-pleasures)).
- What works:
  - It is a real scientific graphic, used without explanation.
  - Its stacked-line form reads as sound, terrain and data at once.
- Lesson: borrow the method (use a real instrument graphic built from Zo's own data), not the image. The image itself is now a cliche.

### Trouvelot astronomical drawings (1882)

- Etienne Trouvelot made large pastels of Jupiter, Saturn, nebulae, aurora and solar prominences. The pastels were shown in 1876, and 15 chromolithographs of them were published in 1882 ([Public Domain Review](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/the-trouvelot-astronomical-drawings-1882), [NYPL](https://live-legacy-admin.nypl.org/blog/2016/08/19/1800s-astronomical-drawings)).
- What works:
  - Scientific observation drawn with an artist's hand.
  - Each plate has a deep field, one luminous subject, and a modest numbered caption ("Plate X").
- Bridge: Jupiter's Great Red Spot is a storm, and Trouvelot drew it. That single fact joins storms and space.

### Luke Howard, On the Modifications of Clouds (1803)

- Howard named cirrus, cumulus and stratus. His plates were engraved (sources disagree on the engraver: Wilson Lowry or Thomas Milton), and some of the landscapes were by Edward Kennion ([Royal Society picture library](https://pictures.royalsociety.org/image-rs-21130), [Public Domain Review](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/essay-on-the-modification-of-clouds/), [Linda Hall Library](https://www.lindahall.org/luke-howard/)).
- What works:
  - Line engraving renders soft vapour with hard parallel strokes.
  - Each plate is a specimen sheet: labelled, numbered, taxonomic.
- Lesson: engraved hatching is the exact texture of the heading Zo loves. A taxonomy of clouds is a taxonomy of ideas.

### Anna Atkins, Photographs of British Algae: Cyanotype Impressions (1843)

- Usually credited as the first photographically illustrated book. It was made by contact-printing specimens in cyanotype, the process later used for architectural blueprints ([Public Domain Review](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/cyanotypes-of-british-algae-by-anna-atkins-1843/), [The Met](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/286656)).
- What works:
  - One Prussian blue, white silhouettes and a handwritten label.
  - Its lineage runs from science, to blueprints, to engineering.
- Lesson: cyanotype blue is the most honest blue for a systems person. It is literally the colour of blueprints.

### Cassette J-cards

- A J-card folds into a front panel, a narrow spine and a short flap that tucks behind the cassette. It carries the cover, the tracklist and the credits ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J-card)).
- What works: the spine and fold lines are visible structure, and everything is small and dense, close to a technical datasheet.
- Lesson: fold lines, crop marks and a spine strip make a page feel like a printed object, not a web page.

### Risograph and halftone

- Riso inks are translucent spot colours. Their published hex values are approximations, and the result depends on the paper and on how layers overlap ([SAIC Everything Riso](https://sites.saic.edu/servicebureau/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2025/03/EverythingRiso_2024.pdf), [stencil.wiki via riso-colors](https://www.npmjs.org/package/riso-colors)).
- What works: misregistration between two inks, a visible dot screen and paper grain. These are honest imperfections.
- Lesson: offset a second ink layer by 2 to 4 px and multiply-blend it. That is the cheapest way to look printed rather than rendered.

### Weather radar and instrument graphics

- NEXRAD reflectivity is measured in dBZ, and there is no single mandated colour table, so a radar image always needs its own scale ([Flying](https://flyingmag.com/how-can-dbz-values-be-negative)).
- Station-model plots, wind barbs and isobars are a full visual grammar that almost no designer uses.
- What works: the legend becomes part of the art, and a scale bar with labelled steps is beautiful in its own right.
- Lesson: a monochrome blue radar sweep, with its own dBZ-style legend relabelled for career intensity, is unexpected and true to Zo.

### Genuinely unexpected extras

- **FitzRoy storm glass and the aneroid barometer dial.** The dial's words ("Stormy, Rain, Change, Fair, Very Dry") are ready-made section labels.
- **The BBC Shipping Forecast.** Its incantatory read-out format ("Viking, North Utsire. Southwesterly 5 to 7, occasionally gale 8 later. Rain. Moderate or good.") is a rhythm for liner notes. Verify the exact format against a real broadcast before quoting it.
- **Factorio blueprint strings and Mass Effect's galaxy map.** Both are diegetic interfaces: a production graph with throughput, and a cluster map with relay lines. They are useful as structure (nodes and edges), not as fan art. These two were not searched; the descriptions come from general knowledge.

## Part 2: three art directions

All three share one technique backbone (see the appendix at the end): an engraved or stippled blue serif title, a paper-grain overlay, and one signature quirk. They differ in which worlds they connect and in the composition.

---

### Direction A: "Low Pressure" (Blue Note meets the weather station)

**Worlds connected:** jazz and hip hop, storms, systems.

**Concept.** A Reid Miles typography cover printed in one blue ink on near-black. Instead of a photograph, the image is a meteorologist's station-model plot: the standardized circle, wind barb and pressure figures that sit on every synoptic chart. The title is set enormous in an engraved serif with Zo's beloved stippled blue fill. It is cropped off the right edge in the Miles manner, and isobars sweep behind it like the grooves of a record. The back is a forecaster's log: each job is a "track" with a timestamp, a pressure reading and a one-word condition. The read-out rhythm is borrowed from the Shipping Forecast. It feels like a calm storm: a system that is measured, named and under control.

**Cover composition (1000x1000).**

- Top 60 percent: the title "LOW PRESSURE" in two stacked lines at about 210px, the second line cropped by the right edge.
- Behind it, five or six concentric isobar curves in thin blue lines, labelled "1004", "1000", "996" in tiny mono.
- Lower left: a single station-model glyph about 180px across, its wind barb pointing up-right.
- Bottom band: "ZO WILLIAMS" in a condensed sans, letter-spaced, with a Blue Note style catalogue line, "ZW 4000 . STEREO".

**Back composition (about 560x1000).**

- Header: "CONDITIONS" in the serif.
- Tracklist: each row has a track number, a role title, an employer in small caps, years in mono, and a right-aligned "pressure" in millibars that falls as the work gets harder and more interesting. That is a running joke.
- Liner notes: three short paragraphs in Shipping Forecast cadence ("Systems, architecture. Northwesterly, increasing. Good, becoming excellent.").
- Footer: a barometer dial strip with the needle on "CHANGE".

**Fonts (Google Fonts).**

- Title: `Libre Caslon Display`. Alternative: `Bodoni Moda` at opsz 96.
- Condensed label: `League Gothic`.
- Data and readings: `IBM Plex Mono`.
- Body notes: `Libre Caslon Text`.

**Palette.**

| Role | Hex |
|---|---|
| Near-black sleeve | `#0B0D12` |
| Blue Note blue (ink) | `#1F5FBF` |
| Stipple highlight | `#7FA8E8` |
| Pale paper (back panel) | `#E9ECEF` |
| Graphite text on paper | `#1A1E26` |
| Hairline | `#3A4558` |

**Textures.** Engraved title fill (snippet 1), stipple noise (snippet 2), and hairline isobars drawn as SVG `path` elements with `stroke-dasharray` on the labels.

**Signature quirk.** Each track on the back carries a tiny wind barb whose feathers encode years in the role: a half feather for each year, a full feather for each two. A legend in the corner explains it, exactly like a real chart key. Readers who notice will decode it, and that is the point.

**Cliche risk and dodge.** Risk: "jazz cover pastiche", meaning a Blue Note costume with no content, or weather imagery read as moody rain. Dodge:

- Never draw rain or windows. Weather appears only as instrument notation (barbs, isobars, millibars).
- Use exactly one ink, so it reads as design discipline, not nostalgia.

### Direction B: "Plate XIV: The Great Red Spot Was Blue" (astronomy plate meets Howard's clouds)

**Worlds connected:** space, storms, connecting ideas.

**Concept.** The cover is a 19th-century astronomical plate in the manner of Trouvelot, reprinted as a cyanotype. It shows a gas giant whose banded storm, the Great Red Spot, is rendered in blue, and it carries a catalogue caption: "Plate XIV. Observations of a persistent storm". It joins Zo's calm-in-a-storm feeling to Mass Effect's star systems. The back is a star chart in the style of an old celestial atlas. Each career role is a star, sized by magnitude, and the stars are joined by constellation lines that are labelled with the ideas carried from one role to the next. The page itself becomes the "connecting ideas across topics" trait. Typography is ECM-quiet: small, spaced and generous.

**Cover composition.**

- Deep cyanotype field.
- A planet disc about 620px across, sitting low and right, so a third of it is cropped by the bottom edge.
- Horizontal storm bands made with displaced turbulence (snippet 3), and one oval vortex.
- Top left: plate number and title in small engraved-style caps.
- Bottom left: "Z. WILLIAMS, OBSERVER", in the IM Fell italic.
- A thin ruled plate border with corner registration ticks.

**Back composition.**

- Upper 55 percent: the star chart. A faint RA and Dec graticule, with eight to ten "stars" (career nodes) drawn as engraved star glyphs with magnitude rings, joined by dotted constellation lines. Each line carries a tiny italic label naming the idea that crossed over (for example "event sourcing", "player feedback loops").
- Lower 45 percent: the tracklist set as a "Catalogue of Objects" table, with columns No., Designation (role), Constellation (domain) and Epoch (years).
- Liner notes as a short "Observer's remarks" paragraph.

**Fonts.**

- Title and captions: `IM Fell English` and `IM Fell English SC`. These are revivals of 17th-century types, so engraving is native to them.
- Atlas labels: `IM Fell DW Pica` in italic.
- Catalogue table: `Cormorant Garamond` at weight 500, with tabular figures where supported, or `IBM Plex Mono` for the numbers.

**Palette.**

| Role | Hex |
|---|---|
| Cyanotype deep | `#0E2A4F` |
| Prussian mid | `#1B4F8A` |
| Cyanotype wash | `#3C78B5` |
| Paper white (silhouette) | `#EEF2F0` |
| Faded ink line | `#9FB8D3` |
| Near-black frame | `#0A0F18` |

**Textures.** Turbulent storm bands (snippet 3), a stippled planet limb (snippet 2, masked by a radial gradient), paper grain (snippet 5), and the graticule as a `pattern` of 0.5px lines.

**Signature quirk.** A small "errata" slip pasted at an angle on the back, with a drop shadow and a slightly lighter paper, which reads: "For 'red' read 'blue' throughout." It is a librarian's joke that explains the title and shows the dry humour.

**Cliche risk and dodge.** Risk: generic "space aesthetic" (a purple nebula, glowing stars, a sci-fi HUD), or the Unknown Pleasures stacked-line plot. Dodge:

- No glow, no purple and no gradient nebula. Everything is engraved line and flat cyanotype.
- The planet is a weather system, not a pretty sphere.
- The star chart uses real atlas grammar (graticule, magnitudes, Bayer-style labels), never a HUD.

### Direction C: "Throughput" (riso J-card meets Factorio and the obi)

**Worlds connected:** systems (Factorio), storms, jazz and hip hop, Japanese sleeves.

**Concept.** A two-ink risograph print, in Medium Blue and Federal Blue on off-white stock, laid out like an exploded cassette J-card. Fold lines, the spine strip and crop marks are all visible. The cover image is a blueprint of a thunderstorm drawn as a factory. Inputs ("warm moist air", "a hard problem") enter on conveyor-like belts, then pass through assemblers labelled "updraft", "condensation" and "charge separation". The output is a single lightning bolt and a calm sky. The joke tells the truth: Zo's job is turning turbulent inputs into a working system. A Japanese-style obi strip wraps the cover's left edge and carries the one-line pitch. Its debt is to Madvillainy (one flat square of colour as identity) and Dilla's Donuts alternate (a plain drawing of a real process).

**Cover composition.**

- Off-white field with visible riso grain.
- The storm-factory diagram fills the centre 70 percent in thin Federal Blue lines. Assembler boxes are hatched, and belts are dashed lines with chevrons.
- Upper right: one flat Medium Blue square of about 120px, the Madvillainy-style identity mark, with "ZW" knocked out in tiny type.
- Left edge: a vertical obi about 90px wide in solid Federal Blue, with the pitch set vertically in white. There is a catalogue number "ZW-001" at the top, and an optional two-character Japanese line `嵐の後` ("after the storm") in `Zen Old Mincho`.
- The whole blue layer is offset 3px from the line layer to fake misregistration.

**Back composition.** Laid out as the J-card interior.

- Narrow spine strip with name and title, rotated.
- Tracklist styled as a production chain: each role is a "recipe" row with inputs on the left, an arrow, and outputs on the right ("Inputs: legacy monolith, 3 teams. Output: event-driven platform, 40 percent fewer incidents").
- "Side A" for the career and "Side B" for the side quests: games, writing and open source.
- Liner notes in small mono, with a "Recorded at" and "Mixed by" credits block (for example "Recorded at: various thunderstorms").

**Fonts.**

- Title: `Instrument Serif`, with the stippled fill applied (snippet 1).
- Diagram labels and recipes: `Space Mono`.
- Credits and body: `Bricolage Grotesque` at weight 400.
- Obi Japanese: `Zen Old Mincho`.

**Palette.** Riso hex values are approximations of the real inks.

| Role | Hex |
|---|---|
| Stock (off-white) | `#F2EFE8` |
| Riso Medium Blue | `#3255A4` |
| Riso Federal Blue | `#3D5588` |
| Riso Blue (accent, sparing) | `#0078BF` |
| Overprint (multiply result) | `#22356B` |
| Near-black type | `#141821` |

The page surround can stay near-black (`#0B0D12`) so the J-card sits on it like a scanned object.

**Textures.** Halftone dots (snippet 4), misregistration through a duplicated layer with `mix-blend-mode: multiply` and a transform offset, riso grain (snippet 5), and hatching inside the assembler boxes from a `pattern` of diagonal lines.

**Signature quirk.** On the back there is a tiny "belt throughput" readout beside the tracklist: "items/min: 15 yrs". There is also a jam marker, a little red-free warning chevron in blue, on the one honest gap or pivot in the career, labelled "rerouted". It shows systems thinking and self-awareness at once.

**Cliche risk and dodge.** Risk: "zine aesthetic" (a twee riso pastiche), or Factorio fan art that only gamers understand. A weaker risk is exoticism from the obi. Dodge:

- Draw the storm-factory in plain flow-diagram grammar, so it reads to a hiring manager as a process diagram first and a game reference second.
- Keep the Japanese to one short, correct phrase, set in a real Mincho, in the functional obi position.

## Appendix: shared texture snippets

All of these are inline SVG or CSS. None of them loads an external asset.

### 1. Engraved or stippled blue serif title (SVG text, the most robust option)

```html
<svg viewBox="0 0 1000 260" width="1000" height="260" aria-label="Low Pressure">
  <defs>
    <!-- engraving: fine horizontal lines that thicken toward the bottom of the glyph -->
    <pattern id="engrave" width="4" height="4" patternUnits="userSpaceOnUse">
      <rect width="4" height="4" fill="#0B0D12"/>
      <rect y="0" width="4" height="2.2" fill="#1F5FBF"/>
    </pattern>
    <filter id="stipple" x="0" y="0" width="100%" height="100%">
      <feTurbulence type="fractalNoise" baseFrequency="0.9" numOctaves="2" seed="7" result="n"/>
      <feColorMatrix in="n" type="matrix"
        values="0 0 0 0 0.50  0 0 0 0 0.66  0 0 0 0 0.91  0 0 0 9 -4.2" result="dots"/>
      <feComposite in="dots" in2="SourceGraphic" operator="in" result="speck"/>
      <feMerge><feMergeNode in="SourceGraphic"/><feMergeNode in="speck"/></feMerge>
    </filter>
  </defs>
  <text x="0" y="210" font-family="'Libre Caslon Display', serif" font-size="230"
        fill="url(#engrave)" filter="url(#stipple)">LOW PRESSURE</text>
</svg>
```

The HTML text alternative uses `background-clip: text` with an inline SVG data URI. This is inline, so it is allowed.

```css
.title {
  font-family: 'Bodoni Moda', serif;
  font-size: 200px;
  color: transparent;
  background:
    repeating-linear-gradient(0deg, #1F5FBF 0 2px, #0B0D12 2px 4px),
    url("data:image/svg+xml;utf8,<svg xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg' width='200' height='200'><filter id='n'><feTurbulence baseFrequency='0.8' numOctaves='2'/><feColorMatrix values='0 0 0 0 .5 0 0 0 0 .66 0 0 0 0 .91 0 0 0 8 -4'/></filter><rect width='100%' height='100%' filter='url(%23n)'/></svg>");
  background-blend-mode: screen;
  -webkit-background-clip: text;
  background-clip: text;
}
```

### 2. Stipple shading on a shape (planet limb, cloud mass)

```html
<filter id="stippleShade">
  <feTurbulence type="fractalNoise" baseFrequency="1.2" numOctaves="1" seed="3"/>
  <feColorMatrix type="saturate" values="0"/>
  <feComponentTransfer>
    <feFuncA type="discrete" tableValues="0 0 0 1"/>  <!-- threshold into hard dots -->
  </feComponentTransfer>
  <feComposite in2="SourceAlpha" operator="in"/>
</filter>
<!-- Apply to a circle filled with the ink colour; mask it with a radial gradient for the falloff. -->
```

### 3. Storm bands and turbulence (gas giant, cloud engraving)

```html
<filter id="storm" x="-10%" y="-10%" width="120%" height="120%">
  <feTurbulence type="turbulence" baseFrequency="0.004 0.04" numOctaves="4" seed="14" result="t"/>
  <feDisplacementMap in="SourceGraphic" in2="t" scale="38" xChannelSelector="R" yChannelSelector="G"/>
</filter>
<g filter="url(#storm)">
  <!-- 12 to 16 horizontal stripes in alternating #1B4F8A / #3C78B5, clipped to the planet circle -->
</g>
```

The anisotropic `baseFrequency` (low x, high y) is what makes horizontal banding instead of marble.

### 4. Halftone dot screen (riso, newsprint)

```css
.halftone {
  background-color: #F2EFE8;
  background-image: radial-gradient(circle at center, #3255A4 0 1.6px, transparent 1.8px);
  background-size: 6px 6px;
  /* fade the dots by masking with a gradient for tonal falloff */
  -webkit-mask-image: linear-gradient(160deg, #000 20%, transparent 85%);
          mask-image: linear-gradient(160deg, #000 20%, transparent 85%);
}
.halftone.rot { transform: rotate(15deg); } /* rotate a second layer to the classic 15/75 degree screen angles */
```

The mask here is a tonal falloff on a single ink, not a colour wash, so it does not break the no-gradient-wash rule.

### 5. Paper grain overlay (whole sleeve)

```html
<svg width="0" height="0" style="position:absolute">
  <filter id="grain">
    <feTurbulence type="fractalNoise" baseFrequency="0.65" numOctaves="3" stitchTiles="stitch"/>
    <feColorMatrix type="saturate" values="0"/>
    <feComponentTransfer><feFuncA type="linear" slope="0.08"/></feComponentTransfer>
  </filter>
</svg>
<div class="grain" style="position:absolute;inset:0;filter:url(#grain);pointer-events:none;mix-blend-mode:multiply"></div>
```

### 6. Riso misregistration

```css
.ink-a, .ink-b { position: absolute; inset: 0; mix-blend-mode: multiply; }
.ink-b { transform: translate(3px, -2px) rotate(0.15deg); }
```

## Recommendation

- **Direction A** is the safest fit with the heading Zo already loves. It reads as "jazz sleeve" at a glance and gets its quirkiness from the weather notation.
- **Direction B** is the most beautiful and the most "connecting ideas". The star chart makes that trait visible.
- **Direction C** is the most atypical and the most systems-forward.

Whichever is chosen, retire the rainy-window sketch entirely.
