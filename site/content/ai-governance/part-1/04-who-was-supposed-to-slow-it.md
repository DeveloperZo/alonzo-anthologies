*Section 4 of 8*

*Every functioning system needs a self-correcting loop. For AI, that was supposed to be government oversight and independent safety research. Here is what that loop is resourced with, and what has happened to it.*

The regulatory bodies with actual enforcement power (electricity grid operators, utility commissions, federal courts) were not designed for AI and have no AI mandate. The bodies that do have an AI mandate have no enforcement power. No actor with authority over both sides has stepped in to close that gap. Five government AI institutes (UK, US, EU, Japan, and Korea) now use the same open-source evaluation framework for model testing.<sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-37" id="part1-fnref-37">37</a></sup> They can run evaluations. They cannot compel access to unreleased models, cannot delay deployment based on their findings, and their results carry no binding enforcement authority. A June 2026 executive order invites labs to give the US government up to 30 days of pre-release access. It is voluntary, and it stops short of licensing or any veto over launch.<sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-46" id="part1-fnref-46">46</a></sup> September showed what voluntary means: Anthropic launched Mythos 5.1 without giving the UK AI Security Institute pre-release access, the first time it had left the institute out.<sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-47" id="part1-fnref-47">47</a></sup> The infrastructure of oversight exists in rudimentary form. The structural power to act on it does not.

One exception has now been demonstrated. In June 2026, days after Anthropic released Claude Fable 5 (the public version of a model it had earlier said was too dangerous to release), the Commerce Department invoked export-control law to suspend the model's availability over a cybersecurity jailbreak. Anthropic pulled it globally, shipped a classifier that by its account was co-developed with government evaluators, and restored access on July 1.<sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-42" id="part1-fnref-42">42</a></sup> Nineteen days offline, by government order: the first time a government forced a frontier model out of deployment worldwide. Two weeks later, OpenAI limited its GPT-5.6 release to a small group of partners approved by the US government, at the administration's request.<sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-48" id="part1-fnref-48">48</a></sup> The enforcement power exists. What June 2026 showed is that it gets used by crisis and by request, one model at a time, not by rule. A brake that engages only after the vehicle is moving is a real brake. It is not a licensing regime.

Now watch a safety policy weaken itself in public. Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (the document governing when Anthropic pauses model development) was updated February 24, 2026 to remove its previous binding pause commitment. Jared Kaplan (Chief Science Officer and Responsible Scaling Officer) told TIME: *"We felt that it wouldn't actually help anyone for us to stop training AI models."* He added that the company "didn't really feel… that it made sense for us to make unilateral commitments… if competitors are blazing ahead."<sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-13" id="part1-fnref-13">13</a></sup> Four further revisions through July 2026 adjusted thresholds and reporting; none restored the binding pause.<sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-13e" id="part1-fnref-13e">13e</a></sup>

Follow that logic through. It ties the safety standard to the lowest bar any competitor is willing to hold, which means the standard gets quietly redefined to match current behavior. This is the dissent paradox: even a lab that built its brand on safety did not hold the line alone once competitors would not.

> [!note]- The liability cases: what settled and what didn't
> Garcia v. Character.AI (M.D. Fla.), the landmark case treating chatbot output as a product rather than protected speech, settled in January 2026 with undisclosed terms and safety-feature commitments for users under 18.<sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-13a" id="part1-fnref-13a">13a</a></sup> A settlement means no appellate precedent was established: the most important legal question (whether AI output is a product subject to product liability) remains formally unresolved. Character.AI itself filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in October 2026.<sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-13f" id="part1-fnref-13f">13f</a></sup> Meanwhile, the wrongful-death suit against OpenAI, Lyons v. OpenAI Foundation (N.D. Cal., the Soelberg case) survived OpenAI's motion to stay in April 2026 and is proceeding toward discovery.<sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-13b" id="part1-fnref-13b">13b</a></sup> The Air Canada chatbot case (Moffatt v. Air Canada, BC Civil Resolution Tribunal, February 2024) produced the first concrete liability judgment. The tribunal held the company responsible for its chatbot's incorrect information, rejecting the argument that the chatbot was a "separate entity."<sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-13c" id="part1-fnref-13c">13c</a></sup> Stanford's AI Index 2026 counts 156 AI-related enforcement actions globally in 2025, up from 43 in 2024.<sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-13d" id="part1-fnref-13d">13d</a></sup> The count is rising. The size is not: the largest single AI-specific fine remains the 15 million euros Italy's regulator levied on OpenAI in December 2024.

> [!note]- The missing price signal: insurance hasn't caught up
> Insurance premiums rise as risk rises. That's the financial feedback signal that normally disciplines risky behavior. For AI, that signal is still largely absent. A March 2026 report by Gallagher Re and MIT<sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-15" id="part1-fnref-15">15</a></sup> found that standard cyber and general-liability policies don't cover AI-native liabilities: hallucinations, biased decisions, and flawed training data fall outside typical coverage. US lawsuits involving generative AI increased 978% between 2021 and 2025,<sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-14" id="part1-fnref-14">14</a></sup> but insurers are still developing frameworks rather than pricing the risk. A specialist market began forming in early 2026 (dedicated AI liability policies with limits above \$25 million, and the first stand-alone product for small businesses in March),<sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-16" id="part1-fnref-16">16</a></sup><sup>,</sup><sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-16a" id="part1-fnref-16a">16a</a></sup> but capacity measured in tens of millions is a rounding error against the liabilities at stake. Mainstream carriers have meanwhile moved the other way: standard generative-AI exclusion forms have circulated since January 2026, and at least one carrier now excludes AI outright across several liability lines.<sup><a href="sources#part1-ref-16b" id="part1-fnref-16b">16b</a></sup> Until insurance pricing reflects AI risk, the market's self-correcting loop remains absent.
